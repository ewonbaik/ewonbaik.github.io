About

I am a PhD candidate in Political Science and International Relations (POIR) at the University of Southern California, based in Los Angeles. My work sits at the intersection of comparative politics, migration, and political behavior. I am currently on the academic job market.

My dissertation examines how home-country political identities persist, transform, or reverse after migration to the United States, with a focus on immigrant communities' political belief consistency across ideology, party identification, and vote choice.

Research

Dissertation: Political Belief Consistency Among Immigrant Communities in the United States
Committee mentor: Prof. Saori Katada

The project draws on a two-wave national survey (a California pilot, N = 887, followed by a national wave, N = 1,000) to test two core hypotheses: a hierarchy of political identifiers in which ideology is less stable than party identification, which is in turn less stable than vote choice; and cohort-based "transfer" effects rooted in respondents' home-country political generation. The dissertation also develops a "security dilemma" reinterpretation of political moderation as strategic ambivalence under interstate rivalry.

My broader research statement integrates the dissertation chapters with a co-authored working paper and a published article, organized around a four-level nested analysis framework.

Teaching

My approach to teaching is shaped in part by four years as a Resident Advisor at Princeton University, where I supported 25–50 first- and second-year students each year and learned what it takes to build community among people who did not choose one another — meeting students where they are rather than where a syllabus assumes them to be.

In grading written work, I grade blind to student identity so that familiarity with a student does not influence the evaluation of their work, and I emphasize detailed, substantive feedback over reducing assessment to clear-cut right and wrong answers.
